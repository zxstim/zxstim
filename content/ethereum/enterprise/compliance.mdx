Ethereum doesn't check anyone's identity, and it doesn't block anyone's transactions. That is why it is [neutral](./neutral-blockspace.mdx). Compliance requirements, such as know-your-customer (KYC) checks, sanctions-list screening or limits on who may hold an asset, are enforced at the layers above. Each layer has a party responsible for it.

## Who controls which layer

| Layer | Who controls it | What they can do |
|---|---|---|
| Ethereum protocol | No one | Blocks no one, freezes no one |
| Token | The issuer | Freeze, block addresses, allow transfers only to verified wallets |
| Layer 2 | The operator | Allow-lists, transaction filtering |
| Application | The app builder | Identity checks, user restrictions |
| Custody | The business or a custodian | Who may sign, how many signatures are needed |

## At the token layer

- **Stablecoins can freeze funds.**
  - The USDC contract has a "blacklister" role: a blocked address can't send, receive or redeem USDC. Under Circle's terms (updated October 2026), Circle may freeze USDC when it judges on its own that there is a link to illegal activity, and must do so when there is a valid order from a government authority.
  - Tether's terms (updated February 2026) also allow it to freeze USDT under the law or when Tether itself considers it necessary.
- **Permissioned tokens (ERC-3643).** This is a finalized standard for securities and tokenized assets. It ensures that:
  - tokens can only be transferred to wallets listed in an identity registry, with confirmation from a trusted verifier;
  - rules can be set, such as a limit on the number of holders;
  - the manager can freeze wallets, force token transfers, or recover tokens to a new wallet belonging to the same person;
  - the whole token can be paused.
- **Regular ERC-20 tokens** can also add allow-lists and similar rules, case by case.

## At the Layer 2 layer

- **Allow-lists:** only approved people can send transactions or deploy contracts. See [Customizing a Layer 2](./customizing-layer-2.mdx).
- **Filtering against a restricted list:**
  - Starting with ArbOS 61, Arbitrum chains can turn on filtering: the sequencer simulates each transaction and rejects any that touch a restricted address, using a list from a data provider. This feature is off by default, and the Arbitrum documentation says it is not meant for Arbitrum One.
  - Robinhood Chain uses this filtering. According to ETHBEAT, the authorized filtering party can force a transaction to fail, including a transaction pushed through Ethereum directly.
  - Some chains built with the ZK Stack filter who may send transactions from Ethereum into the network.
- **The cost:** the operator has the power to block, so the network is less neutral, and the escape hatch may be weakened. The ZKsync documentation warns that users who are blocked later on may have their funds locked.

## Private yet auditable

Every transaction on Ethereum is public. Businesses often need to keep their transactions hidden from competitors while still letting auditors and regulators see them. The tools available today:

- **Selective disclosure and viewing keys:** outsiders can't see the data, but whoever holds a key can see exactly the part they're allowed to.
- **Zero-knowledge proofs:** prove that a customer has passed sanctions screening, or lives in a certain country, without revealing who that person is (per the Ethereum Foundation's guide).
- **Identity attestations:** a party that has done the verification signs a confirmation, and no personal data goes onchain.
- **Privacy Pools:** running on Ethereum mainnet since March 31, 2025. Depositors are screened when they deposit, and there is always a public withdrawal path. In March 2026, the screening party blocked all private withdrawals for 2 hours. This shows that privacy tools still depend on a particular party.
- **Private chains with proofs,** such as Prividium: data is kept private, and only proofs go to Ethereum. See [Customizing a Layer 2](./customizing-layer-2.mdx).
- **Custody using multi-party computation** (MPC) or multisig wallets: no single employee can sign a large transaction alone.

According to ethereum.org, these privacy tools are still new, and many have not been proven at large scale.

## Things to remember

- **Compliance is the responsibility of operators, issuers and app builders,** not the protocol. Know exactly who controls which layer.
- **A legal wrapper is still needed.** The European Investment Bank's digital bond issues still used traditional legal documentation and licensed intermediaries.
- **Regulations differ from country to country** and change quickly. ethereum.org's material on regulation is out of date and barely mentions major legal frameworks such as the European Union's MiCA.
- **In Vietnam,** the Law on Digital Technology Industry (No. 71/2025/QH15) and Resolution No. 05/2025/NQ-CP lay the first foundation for crypto assets. Check against the regulations in force and get legal advice before acting. This is not legal advice.

## Summary

- Ethereum blocks no one. Compliance lives in tokens, Layer 2s, applications and custody, and each layer has a responsible party.
- Tools are available: permissioned tokens (ERC-3643), stablecoin freeze powers, allow-lists and filtering on Layer 2, zero-knowledge proofs, viewing keys.
- Every added control is one more point where users have to trust the operator.
