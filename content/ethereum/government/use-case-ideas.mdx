The Ethereum Foundation (EF) suggests six directions for public-sector use, and states clearly that they are a starting point for exploration, not reports on finished projects. None of them has deployment data yet.

Before going into each one, ask the first question: **does this problem really need a shared record book that no one party controls?** If there's already an agency every party trusts, an ordinary database is usually enough. The EF's own public-sector team also says that talking it through may end in an approach that doesn't use Ethereum.

## Six directions

1. **Monitoring public procurement.** A shared record book of decisions and approvals, so agencies, contractors and auditors can all cross-check it.
   - Hard question: who is allowed to write to it, and which parts of the bid documents are made public.
2. **Checking eligibility while revealing little data.** Proving a condition, such as being old enough, without revealing other personal data. How: zero-knowledge proofs. See [Digital identity and credentials](./identity-and-credentials.mdx).
   - Hard question: an agency must first issue digital documents, and speed on cheap phones (8 to 10 seconds in the EF's test).
3. **Participatory budgeting.** Proposals, votes, allocations and payouts all sit in one trackable record book. How: smart contracts.
   - Hard question: keeping the ballot secret while still being able to count it, with one vote per person. The *State of Private Voting 2026* report (PSE and Shutter) brings together the approaches being tried.
4. **Tracing medicines.** Health agencies check where a medicine came from and where it has been.
   - Hard question: data entered wrong at the start stays wrong, and every link in the supply chain has to record.
5. **Building permits.** Applications, inspection results and decisions are shared between agencies, so citizens know where their application is and what a decision was based on.
   - Hard question: applicants' personal data, and connecting to existing systems.
6. **Climate commitments.** Commitments, progress and evaluation results are recorded so anyone can follow them.
   - Hard question: most of the figures are self-reported.

## Tools that go with them

The EF also lists some mechanisms shared across several directions:

- **Confidential transactions that can still be audited:** outsiders can't see them, but an auditor given a **viewing key** can.
- **Private payments:** each incoming payment uses a **stealth address**, so outsiders can't link the payments together. See [Privacy](../basics/using-ethereum/privacy.mdx).
- **Verifiable official records:** attestations signed by the agency, along with the document's hash. See [What goes onchain](./what-goes-onchain.mdx).

## Summary

- Six directions: public procurement, eligibility checks, participatory budgeting, medicine tracing, building permits, climate commitments. All are still just ideas.
- The first question: do you need a shared record book that no one party controls? If not, a database is enough.
