The Ethereum Foundation's (EF) "Evaluating Ethereum" page asks 11 questions, in three groups. Below are the questions and the EF's answers, summarized, with this wiki's comments where needed. At the end of the page are the points the EF rarely mentions.

## What using Ethereum means in practice

1. **Can sensitive information be kept confidential?** EF: yes. Data stays in existing systems, and privacy-preserving methods let you check something without revealing the data. See [What goes onchain](./what-goes-onchain.mdx).
2. **How much does it cost?** Reading is free; only writing costs a fee. The EF advises counting the cost over the whole lifecycle, including dependence on vendors.
3. **What's the environmental impact?** Since 2022, electricity use has fallen by about 99.95%. The Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance (CCAF) estimates about 7.87 GWh a year in 2026, about as much as 2,900 UK households, for more than 524 million transactions in 2025.
   - Comment: the EF's own guidance gives a different figure, about three times lower. Estimates still differ considerably.
4. **Does it mean giving up control?** EF: no. The agency still decides policy, conditions, compliance, access and operations.
   - Comment: true for the agency's own application. But no agency gets to decide the rules of the network itself (set through [upgrades](../basics/how-it-works/upgrades.mdx)) or the level of fees.

## Compared with other public chains

The EF relies on just one source for this group of questions: OpenZeppelin's technical risk assessment report (2026), which compares six networks.

5. **How far does a software bug spread?** Ethereum has many independent node clients written by different teams, so a bug in one client doesn't break the whole network. Some other networks have only one dominant client. See [Nodes](../basics/how-it-works/nodes.mdx).
6. **Who can take part in running the network?** Ethereum is open to anyone. Some other networks have only a preset, elected or capped group of validators.
7. **Who can change the rules?** Ethereum uses a public proposal process. On some other networks, a foundation or a committee decides.
   - The EF admits: networks with few validators, or run by a foundation, can coordinate and make decisions faster. Ethereum's open process can be slower.

## Compared with private chains

A **private chain** (also called a permissioned chain) is a blockchain that only approved parties may run, usually set up by an agency or a consortium.

8. **Who can take part in running it?** Ethereum: no permission needed. Private chain: approved parties only.
9. **Who can check the ledger for themselves?** Ethereum: anyone. Private chain: only members, or through the consortium's infrastructure.
10. **Who controls the network?** Ethereum: no single body. Private chain: the operator or governing board.
11. **What keeps the network running over the long term?** Ethereum: many independent parties maintain it together. Private chain: it needs ongoing investment; otherwise the agency may have to run it itself or move elsewhere.

## What the EF's material says little about

- **Private chains have strengths too.** Control over who takes part, stable and predictable costs, no need to hold ETH to pay fees, data that isn't public to the whole world, and fast decisions. The EF's comparison with private chains lists only their weaknesses.
- **Layer 2 is a middle option.** [Layer 2s](../basics/how-it-works/layer-2.mdx) have lower fees, can be customized, and write their data back to Ethereum. But most Layer 2s still have a sequencer run by one party and contracts that can be upgraded. The EF's public-sector site doesn't discuss these trust assumptions. Check each network on [ETHBEAT](https://ethbeat.org) (formerly L2BEAT).
- **The first question is still whether you need a blockchain at all.** See [Use-case ideas](./use-case-ideas.mdx).

## Quick comparison

| | Ethereum (mainnet) | Layer 2 | Private chain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who can check for themselves | Anyone | Anyone, if the data is posted to Ethereum | Members |
| Who runs it | Thousands of independent parties | Usually one company running the sequencer | Approved parties |
| Who changes the rules | Public process | The operator, within the limits the contracts allow | Governing board |
| Fees | Paid in ETH, vary with how busy the network is | Much lower | Set by the operator |
| Data | Public | Usually public | Visible to members only |
| Main risks | Volatile fees, public data | The operator, level of maturity | Dependence on the operator, hard for outsiders to check |

## Summary

- The EF proposes 11 questions: about what using it means in practice, compared with other public chains, and compared with private chains.
- Ethereum's strength is that anyone can check it for themselves and no one controls it alone. Private chains are strong on control, stable costs and keeping data confidential.
- Layer 2 sits in the middle: cheap and flexible, but look carefully at who runs it and who can upgrade the contracts.
