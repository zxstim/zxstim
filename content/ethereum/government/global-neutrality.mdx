Every 12 seconds, Ethereum creates a new block. The room in each block set aside for transactions is called **block space**. Anyone who pays the fee can use it. An agency in Hanoi, a company in São Paulo or a citizen in Nairobi all follow the same rules and the same price list. No one gets priority or is shut out because of their nationality.

For the public sector, this is the biggest difference: this shared infrastructure doesn't belong to any one great power, corporation or foundation.

## Why no single party can control it

- **Nodes are everywhere.** According to Ethernodes (November 2026), there are 8,717 execution layer nodes in 95 countries and territories, 24 of them in Vietnam. Anyone can run a node without asking permission. See [Nodes](../basics/how-it-works/nodes.mdx).
- **Block creators take turns at random.** In each 12-second slot, the protocol randomly picks a [validator](../basics/how-it-works/validators.mdx) to propose the block. Validators run machines in close to a hundred countries.
- **There's no single piece of software.** The execution layer has five main clients written by different teams: Geth about 50%, Nethermind about 25%, Besu about 9%, Reth about 8%, Erigon about 7% (according to ethereum.org, May 2026). No team can sneak new rules into the network.
- **Rules only change when the network accepts them.** Each [upgrade](../basics/how-it-works/upgrades.mdx) is discussed in public, and each node operator decides for themselves whether to install the new version.
- **There's no "off switch".** According to Ethereum Foundation (EF) guidance, the protocol has no admin interface for freezing accounts or reversing transactions. The EF also states clearly that it does not run or control Ethereum.
- **It has never stopped producing blocks** since July 30, 2015.

By comparison: a system run by one company or one country, such as a payment network, a cloud service or a private chain, can be switched off by that party, have its terms changed, or be forced to block users under that party's laws. With Ethereum, doing that would mean persuading most of the operators across a great many different countries.

## A real test: the 2022 sanctions

This was the biggest test of Ethereum's neutrality so far, according to ethereum.org:

1. In August 2022, the US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) added Tornado Cash, a mixing contract used to keep transactions private, to its sanctions list.
2. Many MEV-Boost **relays**, nodes run by third parties that pass blocks from block builders to validators, began filtering out related transactions. At the peak (November 2022), about 79% of blocks were built through filtering relays.
3. But the other 21% didn't filter, so those transactions still got onto the chain, just more slowly: the average wait rose from about 12 seconds to about a minute.
4. New non-filtering relays appeared, and no one could force every validator to use a filtering relay. By early 2023, the share of blocks going through filtering relays fell below 50%. For the rest of 2023, it ranged from 27% to 47%. In March 2025, Tornado Cash was removed from the sanctions list.

The lesson: a great power can slow transactions down through companies subject to its laws, but it can't block them outright as long as there are validators elsewhere that don't comply.

Filtering relays still exist. According to MEV Watch, on November 7, 2026, about 16.7% of block deliveries through MEV-Boost relays came from filtering relays. This figure counts relay deliveries, not blocks, so it can't be compared directly with the 79% figure above.

Ethereum is building this guarantee into the protocol itself. The **FOCIL** upgrade (EIP-7805) adds an **inclusion list**: a randomly chosen group of validators publishes pending transactions, and the next block must contain them, or the network will reject that block. FOCIL has been scheduled for the Hegotá upgrade (according to EIP-8081, still a draft). As of November 2026, this is still a plan, with no date.

## The limits of neutrality

- **Nodes are concentrated in a few countries.** The United States has about 30% of nodes, Germany about 14% (Ethernodes, November 2026). A country with many nodes can't change the rules on its own, but it has a louder voice. Many nodes also rent machines from a few large cloud companies.
- **One client runs nearly half the network.** ethereum.org admits that Geth's nearly 50% share is a real weakness: a serious bug there would hit many nodes at once.
- **The layers on top have owners.** Stablecoins are issued by companies and can be frozen under the laws of the country where the company is registered. Layer 2s usually have one company running the sequencer. The same goes for exchanges, custody services and oracles. Ethereum is neutral at the protocol layer; that doesn't automatically hold for these layers.
- **Soft influence still exists.** Upgrades are discussed by the developer community, and people with a loud voice still have influence, even though no one can give orders.

<Callout type="info" title="Note">
Being neutral doesn't mean being above the law. An agency that uses Ethereum still follows its own laws. What Ethereum offers is that no outside party can switch off, change or block the shared infrastructure the agency relies on.
</Callout>

## What it means for a country

- **Shared use without dependence.** A country can rely on Ethereum without needing the goodwill of another country or of a vendor.
- **Checking at home.** An agency can run its own nodes located in Vietnam to check every record for itself, without asking anyone.
- **Choose the layers on top carefully.** Whatever sits on the protocol itself is neutral. Whatever depends on a company, such as a stablecoin, a Layer 2 or an oracle, needs a look at which country's laws that company is subject to.

## Summary

- Ethereum's block space is open to everyone in every country, under the same rules.
- No party can, on its own, switch off the network, change the rules or fully block a valid transaction. In 2022, sanctions only slowed transactions down; they couldn't block them.
- Limits: nodes are concentrated in the US and Germany, one client runs nearly half the network, and the layers on top, such as stablecoins and Layer 2s, still have owners.
