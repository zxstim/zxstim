Picture an official gazette that anyone can read, where no one can edit an article once it's published, and no agency owns the printing press. Each article says clearly who published it and when, with a signature anyone can check. From the public sector's point of view, that is the closest way to understand Ethereum.

## Who runs Ethereum?

No one owns it. Ethereum runs thanks to many independent parties:

- **Nodes**: computers running Ethereum software. Each keeps a copy and checks every block for itself. According to Ethernodes, in November 2026 there were 8,717 execution layer nodes in 95 countries and territories. See [Nodes](../basics/how-it-works/nodes.mdx).
- **Validators**: they lock up ETH as a stake to take part in creating blocks. If they misbehave, they lose their stake. See [Validators](../basics/how-it-works/validators.mdx).
- **The rules** only change through upgrades discussed in public, and each party decides for itself whether to run the new version. See [How Ethereum upgrades](../basics/how-it-works/upgrades.mdx).

The **Ethereum Foundation** (EF) is a nonprofit that funds research and supports the ecosystem. The EF itself states clearly on its public-sector site that it does not run or control Ethereum.

## What the public sector cares about

- **History can't be quietly rewritten.** Each block carries the hash (a "fingerprint") of the block before it. Changing an old record breaks every block after it. After about 15 minutes, a transaction reaches **finality**: to reverse it, an attacker would have to accept losing a very large share of all staked ETH. See [Consensus](../basics/how-it-works/consensus.mdx).
- **Anyone can check for themselves.** Citizens, auditors, journalists and foreign agencies all read the same data, without asking permission or trusting a middleman. Anyone who wants more certainty can run their own node.
- **Neutral, owned by no great power.** No government, company or foundation can, on its own, switch off the network, change the rules or block a country. This helps when many parties need a shared source but don't want to hand it to any one of them: ministries, provinces, countries. See [Neutral infrastructure](./global-neutrality.mdx).
- **Always running.** Mainnet has been running since 2015, with no closing hours. That doesn't mean it has never had problems: in May 2023, the network twice temporarily stopped reaching finality (for about 25 minutes and about an hour), though new blocks were still produced as normal.
- **Rules as code.** [Smart contracts](../basics/how-it-works/smart-contracts.mdx) run on their own according to public rules, for example paying out automatically when a condition is met.
- **Uses little electricity.** Since 2022, Ethereum has used proof of stake instead of mining, and its electricity use fell by about 99.95%.

## What Ethereum can't do

- **It doesn't check the truth.** A blockchain only guarantees that data isn't changed after it's written. Data that was wrong from the start stays wrong.
- **It's not a place for personal data.** Everything on mainnet is public and stays forever. See [What goes onchain, what stays offchain](./what-goes-onchain.mdx).
- **It has no legal validity on its own.** Whether an onchain record has legal validity is up to the law, not the technology.
- **It's not free.** Every write costs a [gas fee](../basics/how-it-works/gas.mdx), paid in ETH.
- **It doesn't replace regulations.** Who holds the keys, who may write, how to correct a wrong entry: those questions still need the agency's own procedures.

<Callout type="info" title="Note">
Most of the material on Ethereum for the public sector is written by the Ethereum Foundation, with the goal of encouraging organizations to use Ethereum. This section draws on that material but keeps a neutral tone: it also covers projects that were stopped, and things the original material rarely mentions. This is not legal advice.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Ethereum is a public record book kept jointly by thousands of independent parties, with no owning agency.
- Strengths for the public sector: global neutrality, history that can't be quietly rewritten, anyone can check it for themselves, and it's always running.
- Limits: it doesn't check whether input data is true, it isn't suited to holding personal data, it has no legal validity on its own, and every write costs a fee.
