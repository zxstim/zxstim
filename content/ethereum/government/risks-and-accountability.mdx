## The key is the official seal

On Ethereum, an agency is identified by an address, and every record is signed with that address's **private key**. See [Addresses and keys](../basics/how-it-works/addresses-and-keys.mdx).

- **Losing the key:** the agency can't publish new records or revoke documents it has issued.
- **Leaking the key:** someone else can publish in the agency's name, and the fake records can't be deleted.
- **Ways to reduce the risk:**
  - use a **multisig** wallet so each important record needs several officials to sign (see [Smart wallets](../basics/using-ethereum/smart-wallets.mdx));
  - keep keys on dedicated devices;
  - have a handover procedure when staff change;
  - have a plan for rotating keys and for announcing the new address.
- **Citizens** who keep documents in a wallet also need a way to recover them if they lose their phone. The EF's pages on OpenCerts, Canada and Bhutan all raise the issue of key management.

## You have to hold ETH to pay fees

Every write costs a [gas fee](../basics/how-it-works/gas.mdx), paid in ETH. In Singapore, the schools taking part in OpenCerts had to pay this fee themselves. For a government agency, this raises some questions:

- **Budgeting:** the price of ETH moves, and so do fees.
- **Accounting:** how to record and settle the ETH spent.
- **Regulation:** whether the agency is allowed to hold crypto assets at all. Check against the current rules on budgets, accounting and crypto assets.

Ways to cut costs: use a Layer 2 with lower fees, bundle many records into one transaction, or have another party pay the fees for you. The party paying the fees still has to hold ETH.

## Legal validity

None of the EF material this wiki has read cites a law that recognizes the legal validity of onchain records. The EF's own guidance notes that central banks treat **settlement finality** as a legal concept, not just a technical matter. Whether a record on Ethereum counts as evidence is decided by law.

## Personal data

Data on mainnet is public and can't be deleted, while the law gives citizens the right to correct their data and to request its deletion. See [What goes onchain, what stays offchain](./what-goes-onchain.mdx).

## Dependence on vendors and data sources

Using a network that no one owns doesn't mean you no longer depend on anyone:

- **Oracles:** US economic figures go onchain through two private companies (Chainlink and Pyth).
- **Data sources:** in Kenya, when the US government shut down, the satellite data was interrupted and one payout was delayed by 18 days.
- **Software and consulting:** the Canadian pilot used software from one company (Bitaccess). The EF's page on this project asks what would happen to the system after the pilot ended.

The EF's public-sector team also puts "institutional independence" as its first principle. Keep the rights to your source code, use open standards, and have an exit plan from the start.

## A pilot isn't operations

- OpenCerts (Singapore) dropped its Ethereum-based model in 2025, after 7 years.
- Canada's pilot stopped in 2019.
- Giga in Rwanda ended in 2025, when the government started paying for internet access itself.
- The results in Kenya were self-reported by the project.

When you hear an example "runs on Ethereum", ask further: is the project still running, which network does it run on, and has it been independently evaluated? Some frequently cited examples actually run on Polygon or Celo, which are separate networks with their own validators.

## Risks of the network itself

- **The rules change through upgrades.** These are planned and discussed in public, but the agency has no say in the decision.
- **Incidents still happen.** In May 2023, the network twice temporarily stopped reaching finality, for about 25 minutes and about an hour, though new blocks were still produced. Whether the "never had downtime" claim in EF material is true depends on how you define "downtime".
- **Bugs in smart contracts.** The agency's code needs an independent audit. The technical prototypes the EF published in November 2026 only run on testnets so far and have not been audited.
- **Layer 2 adds operator risk.** See [Layer 2](../basics/how-it-works/layer-2.mdx).

## Sources with a point of view

The Ethereum Foundation advocates for Ethereum. The EPIC team's brief calls Ethereum the only truly long-term option for public infrastructure. The EF's guidance uses phrases like "most trustworthy". The EF's case studies are more cautious and state their own limits. For big decisions, look for independent assessments as well.

## Summary

- The agency's key is its seal: it needs multisig, dedicated devices, a handover procedure and a key rotation plan.
- Paying fees in ETH raises questions about budgeting, accounting and regulation.
- The legal validity of records is decided by law. Personal data doesn't go on a public chain.
- Many examples are pilots that have stopped. Ask clearly whether the project is still running, and on which network.
