With a regular account, the rules are fixed: a transaction needs one key's signature, and the fee is paid in that account's own ETH. **Account abstraction** is the idea of letting the account itself decide the rules: what it signs with, how many signatures it needs, who pays the fee.

**ERC-4337** is the most common way to do that on Ethereum today. It has been live on mainnet since March 1, 2023, and it needs no change to the protocol: everything is [smart contracts](../how-it-works/smart-contracts.mdx), plus a separate network of bundlers. Most of the [smart wallets](./smart-wallets.mdx) with their own address that you come across today are built on it.

## Why not change the protocol directly?

At the protocol level, a transaction is only valid if it carries a signature from a regular account's key. Changing that rule takes a [network upgrade](../how-it-works/upgrades.mdx), which takes years.

ERC-4337 takes a detour. The smart account doesn't send transactions itself. Someone else sends a regular transaction on its behalf, and a shared contract checks each account's rules before running its instructions.

## The components

| Component | What it is |
|---|---|
| **UserOperation** | The user's instruction: what the account wants to do, plus a signature that follows the account's rules. It's like a transaction, but it isn't sent directly to the network |
| **Bundler** | A node that picks up UserOperations from a separate waiting line, packs many of them into one regular transaction and pays the gas fee up front. ERC-4337 compares this role to a block builder |
| **EntryPoint** | One contract shared by every account. It checks each UserOperation before running it |
| **Smart account** | The contract that holds your assets and sets its own rules: which signatures are valid, how many are needed, what the spending limit is |
| **Factory** | Creates your smart account the first time you use it |
| **Paymaster** | Pays the gas fee for you, sponsored by an app, or accepts your fee in a token such as USDC |

## How does an instruction travel?

<UserOpScene />

1. **The wallet creates a UserOperation** and signs it according to the account's rules: with a passkey, with several keys, or with a limited secondary key. The instruction goes to the bundlers' separate waiting line, not the regular [mempool](../how-it-works/blocks.mdx).
2. **The bundler packages instructions.** It packs many UserOperations into one regular transaction sent to the EntryPoint, and pays the gas fee up front with its own ETH.
3. **The verification loop.** The EntryPoint asks each account whether the signature follows its rules, and asks the account (or the paymaster) whether it can pay the fee. Any instruction that fails is dropped.
4. **The execution loop.** The EntryPoint runs each instruction, then takes the fee from the account or the paymaster to repay the bundler.

The bundler simulates the verification loop before sending, so it doesn't pay gas for instructions that will be dropped. That's why the account's verification step is only allowed to do certain things, under the ERC-7562 rule set.

## The address exists before the account does

The factory uses a fixed formula, so a smart account's address can be calculated before the account is created. You can receive money at that address right away. The account is only actually deployed in the first UserOperation, and the deployment fee is included in that instruction.

<Callout type="warn" title="Warning">
A smart account is a contract on each individual network. The same address on another network is only yours if the account was created there with the same setup. Before receiving money on a new network, check in your wallet that your account works there.
</Callout>

## Paying fees on your behalf

Paymasters are the reason many apps let you transact "for free": the app deposits ETH into a paymaster to pay fees for its users. Some paymasters accept USDC or other tokens as the fee and then pay in ETH themselves. A paymaster decides for itself whom to pay for, for example only users of one app, or only a few instructions per day.

## Trade-offs

- **It costs more gas.** Every UserOperation has to go through the EntryPoint and the account's code. According to the measurements published by the team that wrote the EntryPoint ([version 0.9](https://github.com/eth-infinitism/account-abstraction/blob/v0.9.0/reports/gas-checker.txt), November 2025), a simple UserOperation alone in a batch costs about 77,700 gas, and each additional instruction in the same batch about 41,900 gas. Sending ETH from a regular account costs 21,000 gas. That's why ERC-4337 is used most where gas is cheap, such as on Layer 2.
- **A new address.** To switch over from a regular account, you have to move your assets to a new address. [EIP-7702](./eip-7702.mdx) avoids this, but in exchange the old key keeps full control.
- **Code risk.** The account is a contract, so a bug in its code can lose you money. Many accounts allow their code to be upgraded, so you need to know who holds that power. Use a wallet whose code has been audited and is widely used.
- **It needs bundlers.** Your instruction needs at least one bundler willing to take it. Wallets usually handle this, but if a wallet relies on only one provider, that provider can delay or block your instructions.

## Where is it being used?

According to [BundleBear](https://www.bundlebear.com/erc4337-overview/all) (accessed November 9, 2026), there have been about 1.36 billion UserOperations since launch, from about 72 million accounts. Most of them are outside mainnet: World Chain about 56% and Base about 24% (both are Layer 2s), Polygon about 10%. Ethereum mainnet accounts for only about 0.6%, because fees there are higher.

## The two can be used together

A regular account upgraded with [EIP-7702](./eip-7702.mdx) can delegate to ERC-4337-compatible code, so it can share bundlers and paymasters. The EntryPoint has supported this kind of account natively since version 0.8 (March 2025).

| | ERC-4337 | EIP-7702 |
|---|---|---|
| Address | New address | Keeps the old address |
| Rules | Every instruction has to go through the contract's rules | The original key can bypass the rules |
| Needs a network upgrade? | No | Yes, since Pectra (2025) |

## Coming soon

Two proposals aim to build account abstraction directly into the protocol, so that a separate EntryPoint and bundlers are no longer needed:

- **RIP-7560**, for rollups. As of November 2026 it is still a draft.
- **Frame transactions** (EIP-8141), for mainnet. The proposal has been scheduled for the Hegotá upgrade since August 2026, but it is still being finalized. ethereum.org lists Hegotá as expected in Q2 2027, with no date set.

Until then, ERC-4337 and EIP-7702 remain the two main ways to get a smart wallet.

## Summary

- ERC-4337 (March 2023) brought smart wallets to Ethereum without a network upgrade.
- The wallet sends a UserOperation, a bundler packs many instructions into one transaction, and the EntryPoint checks each account's rules before running anything.
- Paymasters let apps pay fees for you, or let you pay fees in another token.
- The trade-offs: more gas, a new address, and safety that depends on the account's code.
