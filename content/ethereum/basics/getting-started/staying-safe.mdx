Holding your own assets means protecting them yourself. On Ethereum there's no bank to call, no hotline to complain to, and a transaction you've sent can't be taken back. Most people who lose money don't lose it because Ethereum was hacked, but because they were scammed.

<Callout type="warn" title="Important">
No one from ETH.vn, ethereum.org or any wallet will message you first to offer "support." Anyone who messages you privately claiming to be a support agent is almost certainly a scammer.
</Callout>

## Five golden rules

1. **Never share your recovery phrase or private key.** Don't send it to anyone, don't enter it on any website, don't screenshot it, don't save it to the cloud. See [Wallets, accounts and recovery phrases](./wallets-and-accounts.mdx).
2. **Double-check before you send.** Compare the entire recipient address, not just the first and last few characters. The first time you send to an address, send a small test amount.
3. **Read carefully before you sign.** When your wallet asks you to sign or confirm something, read what you're allowing. If you don't understand it, don't sign.
4. **Promises that sound too good are usually scams.** High returns, guaranteed, no risk, decide fast: those are the signs of a scam.
5. **Use a hardware wallet for large amounts.** The keys live on a separate device that never goes online, so malware on your computer or phone can't get them.

## Common scams

### Fake support agents

You post a question in a Telegram, Facebook or Discord group. Right away, someone messages you privately, claiming to be an "admin" or "tech support," and offers to help you "sync your wallet" or "recover your account." In the end, they'll ask for your recovery phrase or send you a link to enter it.

**How to avoid it:** real support never messages you first, and never needs your recovery phrase.

### Giveaways and "send one, get two"

An account impersonating a celebrity or a major project promises: "Send 1 ETH, get 2 ETH back." They often use fake livestream videos or hijacked social media accounts.

**How to avoid it:** no one gives away free money. Whatever you send is gone.

### Fake websites and ads

Scammers build websites that look exactly like a real wallet or app, with just a few characters different in the domain name, then run ads so the fake site shows up at the top of search results. The fake site asks you to enter your recovery phrase or to sign a transaction that empties your wallet.

**How to avoid it:** bookmark the sites you use often and only open them from your bookmarks. Don't click on ads or unfamiliar links in messages and emails.

### Airdrops and unknown tokens

An unknown token suddenly shows up in your wallet, along with an invitation to visit a website to "claim your reward." When you connect your wallet and sign, the scammer gets permission to withdraw your assets.

**How to avoid it:** ignore unknown tokens. Don't visit any website mentioned in a token's name or description.

### Address poisoning

A scammer creates an address whose first and last few characters match an address you often transact with, then makes a zero-value transaction so the fake address shows up in your transaction history. Next time, if you copy an address from your history, you might copy the fake one by mistake.

<PoisoningScene />

Wallets usually shorten addresses to the first and last few characters, which are exactly the parts the scammer made look the same. It's the middle that's different.

**How to avoid it:** don't copy addresses from your transaction history. Use your wallet's address book and compare the entire address.

### Investment groups and fake exchanges

You're invited into a group of "experts" and told to deposit money into an unfamiliar exchange or app. At first you see profits and can withdraw a little. Once you deposit more, the exchange locks your account or demands extra "withdrawal fees" or "taxes."

**How to avoid it:** be wary of strangers who invite you to invest, especially when they promise steady returns or push you to deposit more.

### "ETH2" tokens and fake upgrades

Every time Ethereum upgrades, scammers invite you to "swap your ETH for the new ETH." There is no new ETH. Ethereum upgrades happen automatically; you don't need to do anything.

## Token spending permissions

When you use some apps, your wallet asks whether you allow that app to spend your tokens (an approval). If you grant unlimited permission and the app gets hacked or turns out to be a scam, your tokens can be drained.

<ApprovalScene />

The allowance is recorded in the token contract, not in the app, so it stays in place after you stop using the app, until it's used up or revoked.

- Only approve the exact amount you need, if your wallet offers that option.
- Regularly review and revoke permissions you no longer use, for example with [Revoke.cash](https://revoke.cash).

## General online safety

Your digital assets are only as safe as your devices and accounts:

- **Strong, unique passwords** for every service. Use a password manager.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication** (2FA) with an authenticator app or a security key instead of SMS.
- **Remove browser extensions you don't need.** Malicious extensions can read everything you do in your browser.
- **Update your operating system and apps** regularly.

## If you think you've been scammed

1. **Stop right away**: don't send any more money and don't sign any more transactions.
2. If your recovery phrase was exposed: **create a new wallet** on a clean device and immediately move your remaining assets to it. Consider the old wallet lost.
3. If you gave spending permission to an unfamiliar site: **revoke the permission** right away.
4. Tell your family, the community and the authorities. Don't trust anyone who promises to "get your money back" for a fee: that's often a second scam.
