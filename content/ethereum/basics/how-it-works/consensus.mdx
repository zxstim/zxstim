A [blockchain](./blockchain.mdx) only means something if every node keeps the same copy. But there's no central server deciding which copy is right. Nodes receive transactions in different orders, the network is sometimes slow, and some nodes may lie on purpose.

The way the whole network comes to agree is called a **consensus mechanism**: the full set of rules, rewards and penalties that helps nodes agree on the state of the blockchain.

## The Byzantine Generals Problem

In 1982, three computer scientists, Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease, described this problem with a story known as the [Byzantine Generals Problem](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/byz.pdf):

- Several armies surround a city. If they attack together, they win. If they attack piecemeal, they lose.
- The generals can only communicate through messengers.
- Some of them are traitors. A traitor can tell one general one thing and another general something else.

How can the loyal generals still settle on the same plan?

<ByzantineScene />

The solution has three key ideas, and blockchains use exactly these three:

1. **Send to everyone.** Every vote is sent to everyone directly, not passed hand to hand through middlemen.
2. **Sign every vote.** With [digital signatures](./digital-signatures.mdx), nobody can fake someone else's words, and anyone who says two contradictory things gets caught red-handed.
3. **Decide by an overwhelming majority.** On a network like the internet, where messages can arrive late, a group can only reach consensus safely if more than 2/3 of its members are honest. That's why Ethereum needs 2/3 of the votes.

A system that keeps working correctly even when some of its members cheat is said to have **Byzantine fault tolerance**.

## Who gets to vote?

In the story, the number of generals is fixed. On an open network like Ethereum, anyone can join, and one person can create thousands of fake identities to vote as if they were a whole crowd. This kind of cheating is called a **Sybil attack**.

So votes can't be counted per head. Each vote has to cost something that's hard to fake. According to ethereum.org, that's the job of proof of work and proof of stake: they resist Sybil attacks and pick who gets to write the next block. They come paired with a fork-choice rule so that all the nodes pick the same chain.

## Proof of work

In 2009, Bitcoin introduced the first practical solution: **proof of work**.

- **Miners** have their computers guess billions of numbers every second, looking for one that makes the block's hash smaller than a given threshold. Finding it is very hard, but checking it is very easy.
- Whoever finds it first gets to add the block and collect the reward. Nodes follow the chain with the most computing work behind it.
- Here, a vote is computing power. To cheat, you'd need to control more than half of the whole network's computing power, which is called a **51% attack**.

Ethereum used proof of work from its launch in July 2015. The price was electricity: estimates from before 2022 put Ethereum's use at tens of terawatt-hours of electricity per year. Bitcoin still uses proof of work today.

## Proof of stake

The idea of **proof of stake** has been around for a long time, but it took years of research before it was safe enough for real use:

- **December 1, 2020:** the Beacon Chain launched, running proof of stake alongside Ethereum.
- **September 15, 2022:** **The Merge** switched Ethereum over to proof of stake for good. No history was lost, and ETH holders didn't have to do anything.

Miners were replaced by [validators](./validators.mdx), people who lock up ETH as a stake. In each 12-second slot, one validator is chosen at random to propose a block, and the other validators check it and vote. Votes are weighted by the amount of ETH staked. Once two consecutive checkpoints receive votes from 2/3 of all staked ETH, the block reaches **finality**.

What happens if an attacker controls a lot of the stake?

| The attacker controls | What they can do |
|---|---|
| More than 1/3 of the stake | Delay finality |
| More than 1/2 of the stake | Censor transactions, control the upcoming blocks |
| More than 2/3 of the stake | Reverse even blocks that have already reached finality |

But every time an attack is detected, the attacker gets slashed and kicked out. To try again, they'd have to buy ETH all over again and get back in line from the start. With proof of work, an attacker keeps their mining machines to use again. And if everything else fails, the community can still coordinate to split the chain and cut out the attacker.

## Quick comparison

| | Proof of work | Proof of stake |
|---|---|---|
| Who creates blocks | The miner who solves the puzzle fastest | A randomly chosen validator |
| What you have to put in | Mining machines and electricity | ETH locked up as a stake |
| Block time | Random (about 13 seconds on Ethereum back then) | Exactly 12 seconds per slot |
| Finality | Only grows more certain as more blocks pile on top | Clear-cut, after about 13 to 19 minutes |
| Ethereum's energy use | Very high | Down about 99.95% after The Merge |
| What cheaters lose | Electricity costs, but they keep their machines | They get slashed and removed from the network |

<Callout type="warn" title="Warning">
There is no "ETH2" coin. The Merge didn't turn your ETH into anything else. Anyone inviting you to "upgrade" or "swap" your ETH for ETH2 is a scammer.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The Byzantine Generals Problem asks how a group can agree when some members lie. The solution: send to everyone, sign every vote, and require more than 2/3 to be honest.
- On an open network, a vote has to cost something that's hard to fake. Proof of work uses electricity; proof of stake uses staked ETH.
- Ethereum used proof of work from 2015 to 2022, then switched to proof of stake, cutting its energy use by about 99.95%.
