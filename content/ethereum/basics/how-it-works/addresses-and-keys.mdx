An Ethereum address looks like this: `0x71C7656EC7ab88b098defB751B7401B5f6d8976F`. You give it to other people so they can send you money, like a bank account number. But no bank issues this address. Your wallet calculates it on its own from a pair of keys.

## You hold keys, not money

Your assets always live on the Ethereum blockchain, not on your phone. What you actually hold is your **private key**: the one key that lets you spend the assets held at your address.

So it's worth telling these apart:

- **Account**: an entry on Ethereum with an address, a balance and a few other pieces of information.
- **Wallet**: an app that helps you keep your keys and use your account. Losing your phone doesn't mean losing your account, as long as you still have your recovery phrase.

Ethereum has two types of accounts. The kind you use every day is controlled by a private key (ethereum.org calls it an externally owned account, or **EOA** for short). The second kind is a **contract account**: it's controlled by smart contract code and has no private key.

## From recovery phrase to address

<KeysScene />

1. **Recovery phrase.** When you create a wallet, it generates a very large random number and writes it down as 12 or 24 words. One phrase can create many accounts, so wallets usually let you add more accounts without a new phrase.
2. **Private key.** A 256-bit number, written as 64 hexadecimal characters. There are so many possible keys that two people will almost never end up with the same one.
3. **Public key.** Calculated from the private key with the elliptic curve digital signature algorithm (ECDSA). This calculation only goes one way.
4. **Address.** Take the public key, hash it with Keccak-256, keep the last 20 bytes, then add `0x` in front. The result is 42 characters: `0x` followed by 40 hexadecimal characters.

None of these steps can be reversed. Knowing an address or a public key, nobody can work out the private key. That's why you can post your address online and still be safe.

<Callout type="error" title="Caution">
Anyone who has your private key or recovery phrase has full control of your assets, and nobody can get them back for you. Don't take a photo of it, don't save it to the cloud, don't type it into any website and don't give it to anyone, including people who claim to be tech support.
</Callout>

## Uppercase and lowercase in addresses

You'll see the same address written sometimes in all lowercase and sometimes with a mix of uppercase letters. Both point to the same account. The mixed-case version is a way to catch typos (a checksum): if you mistype a character, your wallet will usually notice.

## Check the address before you send

Transactions on Ethereum can't be reversed. Send to the wrong address and the money is gone. So:

- **Copy and paste**; don't type it by hand.
- **Compare many characters**, not just the first and last few. Scammers create addresses that match the beginning and end of an address you use often, then send you a tiny transaction so the fake address shows up in your transaction history. Don't copy addresses from your transaction history.
- **Send a small test amount** first when you're sending a large sum to a new address.

<Callout type="warn" title="Warning">
One address can be used on Ethereum and on many other networks that use the same format (such as Layer 2s). The balance on each network is separate. When depositing to or withdrawing from an exchange, pick the right network.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Your wallet derives the private key from the recovery phrase, the public key from the private key, and then the address from the public key.
- Each step only goes one way: you can share your address, but your keys and recovery phrase must stay secret.
- An address is `0x` followed by 40 characters. Check it carefully before you send.
