Each Ethereum [block](./blocks.mdx) can only hold a limited number of transactions, so that ordinary people can still run a node. When lots of people use it at once, fees go up. **Layer 2** is the fix: run transactions somewhere else so they're fast and cheap, while still relying on Ethereum for security.

According to ethereum.org, a Layer 2 is a separate blockchain that extends Ethereum and inherits Ethereum's security guarantees. Ethereum is then called **Layer 1**. Networks that handle their security some other way, such as sidechains, aren't counted as Layer 2 by ethereum.org (see [the last section](#what-else-is-there-besides-rollups)).

## Rollups: do the work outside, record it on Ethereum

Most Layer 2s today are **rollups**. Here's how they work:

1. You send a transaction to the Layer 2. A **sequencer** puts it in order and confirms it almost instantly.
2. Lots of transactions are bundled into a **batch** and compressed. An ETH transfer takes up about 112 bytes on Ethereum, but only about 12 bytes in a rollup batch.
3. The batch's data is posted to Ethereum. Anyone can download it and recalculate every balance on the Layer 2 for themselves.
4. Ethereum is convinced that the batch is correct, in one of the two ways described below.

Because the data lives on Ethereum, reversing a transaction on a rollup would mean reversing Ethereum itself.

## Why the data has to be on Ethereum

The key to a rollup is **data availability**: the transaction data is posted publicly to Ethereum, and anyone can download it. Because of that:

- Anyone can recalculate the balances on the Layer 2 for themselves, and point out any mistakes.
- You can always prove how much you have so you can withdraw to Ethereum, even if the operator won't cooperate.
- If the sequencer stops running, someone else can rebuild the state from the data on Ethereum and keep going.

The data is only half of it. The other half is the question: how does Ethereum know the batch's computed result is correct? The two types of rollup answer that question in two different ways.

## Optimistic rollups: correct until proven wrong

An **optimistic rollup** gets its name because it assumes by default that every batch is correct. Whoever posts a batch sends Ethereum the result (the new state after the batch) along with a **bond**, but no proof of any kind.

Then comes the **challenge period**. During this time, anyone who recalculates the result and finds it wrong can submit a **fraud proof**:

1. The two sides split the disputed part in half, then split the part they still disagree on in half again, and keep going until they only disagree on **a single computation step**.
2. A contract on Ethereum computes that step itself and rules on which side is right.
3. The side that's wrong loses its bond. If the batch was wrong, it's thrown out.

Thanks to this halving, Ethereum doesn't have to recompute the whole batch, just a single step.

What this means:

- **It only takes one honest person.** No matter how many parties cheat, a single honest watcher who challenges in time is enough to block a bad batch. On the other hand, if no one is watching, a bad batch could slip through.
- **Withdrawing to Ethereum means waiting out the challenge period.** ethereum.org says about 7 days; the actual figure depends on the network. According to ETHBEAT (formerly L2BEAT), as of November 2026, Arbitrum One's challenge period was 6 days and 8 hours. If you want it faster, you can pay a fee to a service that fronts you the money: they pay you on Ethereum right away, then wait to receive your withdrawal themselves.
- **Ethereum apps run easily.** An optimistic rollup's virtual machine is almost identical to the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM), so contracts and tools written for Ethereum run almost unchanged.

Examples: Arbitrum One, OP Mainnet, Base.

## ZK-rollups: with a mathematical proof attached

A **ZK-rollup** sends each batch along with a **validity proof**: a mathematical proof that the new state really is the result of running the transactions in the batch. A contract on Ethereum checks the proof before accepting the batch. If the proof is wrong, the batch is rejected. Since no bad batch can be accepted, ZK-rollups don't need a challenge period.

What's special is that checking a proof is much faster and cheaper than recomputing every transaction. One proof can even confirm many other proofs (a **recursive proof**), so a single check on Ethereum can confirm many Layer 2 blocks.

What this means:

- **Security rests on math,** not on someone watching. You don't need to check the chain yourself to keep your money safe.
- **Withdrawals are faster.** You just wait for the proof to be created and checked. Some networks add an extra safety delay: according to ETHBEAT, as of November 2026, ZKsync Era waits an extra 1 day.
- **Creating proofs is expensive.** It takes powerful computers, sometimes specialized hardware. That cost is built into users' fees. And if only a few parties can afford to create proofs, the network can easily end up concentrated around them.
- **Ethereum apps are harder to run.** Proving every EVM operation mathematically is very complex. Many ZK-rollups can now do it; this is called a **zkEVM**.

Examples: ZKsync Era, Linea, Scroll, Starknet.

There are two common kinds of proof:

- **SNARK:** small proofs that are cheap to check, but they need a **trusted setup** at the start. The setup is safe as long as at least one honest participant deletes their secret part.
- **STARK:** no trusted setup needed, and believed to be safe against quantum computers. In exchange, the proofs are bigger, so checking them on Ethereum costs more.

Many networks combine the two: they create a STARK proof and then wrap it in a SNARK so it's cheaper to check. According to ETHBEAT, ZKsync Era and Scroll do this.

<Callout type="info" title="Note">
"ZK" (zero-knowledge) doesn't mean private. A ZK-rollup's transaction data is still posted publicly to Ethereum.
</Callout>

## Comparing the two types of rollup

| | Optimistic rollup | ZK-rollup |
|---|---|---|
| How it knows a batch is correct | Assumed correct by default. Anyone who spots a mistake submits a fraud proof | Every batch comes with a validity proof, which Ethereum checks before accepting it |
| Security relies on | At least one honest person watching and challenging in time | Math |
| Withdrawing to Ethereum | Wait out the challenge period, usually a few days to a week | Wait for the proof to be created and checked, plus a safety delay if the network sets one |
| Main cost | Posting data to Ethereum | Posting data, plus the cost of creating proofs |
| Running Ethereum apps | Easy, almost unchanged | Harder; many networks can now do it (zkEVM) |
| Examples | Arbitrum One, OP Mainnet, Base | ZKsync Era, Linea, Scroll, Starknet |

<Callout type="info" title="The line is blurring">
Some optimistic rollups have started using ZK proofs in their challenge process. Since May 2026, Base has combined ZK proofs with attestations from secure hardware (TEE). The challenge period is 5 days with one kind of proof, and drops to 1 day when both kinds confirm. ETHBEAT still classifies Base as an optimistic rollup (data as of November 2026).
</Callout>

## What else is there besides rollups

Not every network connected to Ethereum is a rollup. To tell them apart quickly, ask two questions: **where does the transaction data live**, and **what guarantees the result is correct**.

| Type | Where the data lives | What guarantees the result is correct | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optimistic rollup | On Ethereum | Fraud proofs | No one is watching and challenging in time |
| ZK-rollup | On Ethereum | Validity proofs | Only a few parties can afford to create proofs |
| Validium | Outside Ethereum | Validity proofs | Data is withheld and funds get stuck |
| Optimium | Outside Ethereum | Fraud proofs | Data is withheld and no one can challenge |
| Sidechain | On the sidechain itself | The sidechain's own consensus | A majority of the sidechain's validators collude |
| Plasma | Held by the operator | Fraud proofs | The operator withholds data |
| State channel | Kept by the parties in the channel | Signatures from every party in the channel | Not being online to dispute an old state |

ethereum.org only treats rollups and state channels as true Layer 2s. Sidechains, validiums and plasma get their security from somewhere else, not from Ethereum.

A **validium** uses validity proofs like a ZK-rollup, but its transaction data isn't posted to Ethereum. The data is held by a group of parties, usually called a **data availability committee**. The operator can't create false balances, because Ethereum still checks the proofs. But if the data is withheld, you can't prove how much you have in order to withdraw, and your funds could get stuck. In exchange, fees are cheaper, and the data isn't made public to everyone, though whoever holds the data still sees all of it.

An **optimium** (the name ETHBEAT uses) is like an optimistic rollup, but its data also lives outside Ethereum. Without the data, outsiders can't create fraud proofs, so security depends on whoever holds the data.

A **sidechain** is an independent blockchain with its own consensus mechanism and rules, connected to Ethereum by a two-way bridge. It doesn't post data or proofs to Ethereum, so it doesn't inherit Ethereum's security. Examples according to ethereum.org: Polygon PoS, Gnosis Chain.

**Plasma** is an older design. The data sits with the operator, and only a summary is posted to Ethereum. If the operator cheats and withholds the data, everyone has to race to withdraw their funds at the same time (a "mass exit"), which could clog Ethereum itself. Plasma also can barely run smart contracts. According to ethereum.org, rollups have replaced plasma.

A **state channel**, and its simpler form, the **payment channel**: a fixed group of people lock funds into a [multisig](../using-ethereum/smart-wallets.mdx) contract on Ethereum, then transact with each other outside it by jointly signing each new state. Only opening and closing the channel need transactions on Ethereum. The downsides: the number of people in the channel is fixed, the funds are locked for as long as the channel is open, and you have to stay online (or have someone watch for you) to dispute it if the other side submits an old state. That's why, on Ethereum, channels are used less than rollups.

A network's type can change. For example, in August 2026, Arbitrum Nova stopped using a data availability committee, switched to posting its data to Ethereum, and was reclassified by ETHBEAT from an optimium to an optimistic rollup.

## Blobs: cheap data space for rollups

Posting data to Ethereum is a rollup's biggest expense. Since the Dencun upgrade in 2024 (EIP-4844), rollups post their data in **blobs**: a type of data that travels alongside a block, with its own fee market, and much cheaper than before.

Blobs aren't kept forever. Nodes keep blobs for about 18 days, long enough for anyone to download and check them. The Fusaka upgrade in December 2025 added PeerDAS, which lets each node keep only part of the blob data, paving the way for more blobs per block.

## Trade-offs to know about

- **The sequencer is usually run by a single party.** That party can delay or refuse your transactions. Many rollups let you force your transaction through Ethereum after a waiting period, but that route is more complicated. ETHBEAT shows which networks have this escape hatch.
- **Layer 2 is still young.** The level of security varies between networks. The [ETHBEAT](https://ethbeat.org) site rates each network: who can upgrade the contracts, whether the proof system is live yet, and it places each rollup at Stage 0, 1 or 2. The higher the stage, the less power the operator has to intervene. See [Layer 2 for enterprise](../../enterprise/layer-2-for-enterprise.mdx) for what each stage means.
- **The name doesn't tell you everything.** Many networks call themselves "Layer 2" but are actually validiums, optimiums or sidechains. Check a network's type on ETHBEAT before sending a large amount.

<Callout type="warn" title="Warning">
Funds on a Layer 2 live on that network, not on Ethereum. To move them back and forth, you have to use a **bridge**. When depositing to or withdrawing from an exchange, pick the right network: sending on the wrong network can lose you money. See [One address, many networks](../getting-started/wallets-and-accounts.mdx#one-address-many-networks).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Layer 2 runs transactions outside Ethereum and then writes the data back to Ethereum, so it's fast and cheap while still relying on Ethereum's security.
- Rollups post their data to Ethereum, so anyone can check it for themselves and you can always withdraw your funds.
- Optimistic rollups assume batches are correct and wait out a challenge period, usually a few days to a week. ZK-rollups attach a validity proof so they don't need a challenge period, but creating proofs costs more.
- Validiums and optimiums keep their data outside Ethereum. Sidechains handle their own security. These types don't fully inherit Ethereum's security.
- Since 2024, rollups have posted their data in blobs, which is much cheaper.
- Check each network's type and stage on ETHBEAT before sending a large amount.
